## Registered Hata collection

The registered [Tottori Prefectural Museum page 「かみそり狐（智頭町波多）」](https://www.pref.tottori.lg.jp/265927.htm) is directly readable, with an oral-story transcription and an audio player. It states collection on 1985-08-15 in Hata, Chizu Town; the storyteller is identified only as born in Meiji 40 (1907). In the transcription, the fox is explicitly called 「おさん狐」. It becomes a young woman, fools people in a household, and a young man who follows and exposes the deception is rescued by a monk; after sleep he finds himself in a field and learns that his hair has been torn out. The story concludes that O-san fox makes people bald. This supports the registered claim's account of the Tottori page, but the recording itself was not listened to in this review. The page's web publication date and the underlying field-record archive/catalog identifier are not stated in the inspected text.

The page is a museum-hosted direct transcription of a dated field collection, not a printed original publication. Treat it as a primary collection record for the reported 1985 narrative, subject to checking the embedded audio and the museum's record provenance. The collection date is not the story's event date or the first appearance of the name.

## Same-name comparison record

A separate [Tottori Prefectural Museum page 「喜助とおさん狐（三朝町大谷）」](https://www.pref.tottori.lg.jp/266696.htm), lines 122–151, reports collection on 1988-08-20 in Otani, Misasa Town, and a storyteller also described as born in Meiji 40. It uses 「おさん狐」 for a fox that accepts a short-term marriage arrangement from a man named 喜助, is tricked with cooked plant roots presented as food, then complains to the household and leaves. The page calls this a 「単独伝承」 after noting that 関敬吾's folktale taxonomy does not provide a matching parallel. That editorial assessment belongs to this 1988 record; it does not prove that the 1985 Hata story or other “O-san fox” stories are historically independent or unique.

The two pages identify different towns and collection dates, tell materially different plots, and give no cross-reference or identification of one continuing fox-persona. Their shared name and the same rounded storyteller birth year (both “Meiji 40”) do not establish the same teller or the same being. Do not merge `hata_no_osangitsune` with the Misasa story/entity on the name alone. The Hata source's 「おさん狐」 functions as a named story-character in a local tale; its relation to a named individual outside that narrative is unestablished.
